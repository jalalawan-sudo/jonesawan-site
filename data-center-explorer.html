Rate impacts of large loads: a decision tree

Adapted from ESIG's framework. Hover an outcome box for real-world citations. ⓘ

Step 1 · Generation needs
Is the utility exposed to wholesale capacity/energy market prices?
↓ yes
Do market prices stay flat or fall with the new load added?
↓ no
Is existing load fully hedged against near-term price volatility?
↓ no
Rates increase for existing customers
Real-world instance: HBS/Kaps et al. 2026 (+$1.70/MWh residential, ~4% of the price increase); Brookings 2026 (correlational, Dominion disconnections)
Step 2 · Network capacity needs
Is system headroom available?
↓ yes, or can be added cost-effectively
Are marginal expansion costs less than embedded (average) costs?
↓ yes
Can incremental cost be recovered from the new customer/class over the asset lifetime?
↓ yes
Rates are not affected or decrease
Real-world instance: Watten/Bistline/Blanford 2026 (causal IV design, -3.5% to -6%); Brattle/Alliant 2026 (IA/WI, net neutral-to-protective)
Additional factors that widen or narrow the impact (ESIG, Box 2)
  • Load forecast accuracy — how well the timing of the new load lines up with planned system upgrades.
  • Large-load tariff design — cost-allocation practices and tariff terms shape how costs or savings reach existing customers.
  • Customer load profile — how much the load coincides with system peak drives how much capacity must be reserved for it.
  • Distribution system impacts — loads connecting at distribution voltage may need dedicated substation/feeder upgrades.
  • Economic development rates — incentive rates can create a gap between the cost of serving a load and the revenue it pays.
  • Frequency of rate cases — costs added between rate cases aren't reflected in rates until the next proceeding.
  • Multi-year rate plans — regulatory lag can delay when a large load's rate impact actually shows up.
  • Other load growth — growth from other customers dilutes the per-unit impact of any single large-load class.

A data center policymaker's guide (draft)

What the table above implies procedurally — not a position on any docket. ⓘ

  1. Reject both absolute claims on sight. Two causal designs in the table above reach opposite signs using comparably serious methods — that is the honest state of the evidence, not a contradiction to resolve. ⓘ
  2. The deciding variable is institutional, not technological. Effects concentrate in investor-owned utilities and deregulated markets; cooperatives and regulated utilities show little effect. ⓘ
  3. Match the unit of analysis to the question. State-level comparisons cannot detect a cost shift that happens at the utility-feeder level. ⓘ
  4. Tariff terms are the actual, measurable lever. National mean customer-protection score: 1.94/5; 51% score 0–1. ⓘ
  5. Demand flexibility is a genuine hedge — only if contractual. Voluntary courtesy offers do not move large operators. ⓘ
  6. Demand a forward-looking study before relying on a backward-looking one. Most literature above measures 2010–2025, not the next wave at current costs. ⓘ
Does data center growth raise your electricity bill? It depends on the tariff it signs — weak terms shift stranded costs to you, strong terms make the data center pay its own way. ⓘ

The customer-protection terms that decide the answer

Three tariff elements, scored 0–5, determine whether the large load or the general ratepayer base bears the cost if demand forecasts are wrong ⓘ.

TermWhy it mattersStrong standard observed
Minimum billing demandSets the floor the data center pays even if it uses less — protects against under-recovery on reserved capacity.85% of contract capacity, ratcheted to the greater of that or 80% of the highest billing demand in the prior 11 months (Indiana Michigan Power)
Contract term & rampProtects against stranded assets if the load leaves early.12–15 years, ramp ≤4 years with stepwise floors (AEP Ohio, Dominion VA GS-5)
Exit feeDecides who pays for capacity built for a customer that then leaves.3 years of minimum charges plus net book value of incremental utility-funded facilities (AEP Ohio + Xcel MN combined standard)
CIAC (contributions in aid of construction)Decides who advances the capital for new infrastructure.Required upfront, customer funds planning and interconnection studies
