The underlying research

This map draws on the same facility panel behind a county-level re-examination of whether data centers shift costs onto ordinary ratepayers — the question the rest of this page is built around.

The Causal Bar E3 Did Not Clear: A County-Level Re-Examination of Data Centers and Rates ↗
SSRN preprint, May 2026. 1,506 US facilities and the full 77-tariff SEPA/NCCETC database; host counties carry higher PM2.5, greenhouse gases, water withdrawals and minority population share, and most large-load tariffs score poorly on customer protection — descriptive findings, not a causal claim.

Does data center growth raise your electricity bill? It depends — mainly on the tariff terms the data center signs: the minimum demand charge, the contract term and ramp schedule, the exit fee, and whether the utility requires contributions in aid of construction (CIAC) for new infrastructure. A data center on a weak tariff (low minimum demand, short term, no exit fee) can leave other customers holding stranded costs if it leaves or underuses capacity; a data center on a strong tariff (high minimum demand, 10–15 year term, real exit fees) pays its own way and can even subsidize the system by covering fixed costs other customers would otherwise carry. Anyone who tells you the answer is universally yes or universally no is selling a conclusion, not reporting a finding — see the Literature Review tab for what the actual studies show.

The customer-protection terms that decide the answer

Three tariff elements, scored 0–5, determine whether the large load or the general ratepayer base bears the cost if demand forecasts are wrong ⓘ.

TermWhy it mattersStrong standard observed

Rate impacts of large loads: a decision tree

Adapted from ESIG's conceptual framework (Figure 2 below), with the two end states annotated against what the literature review on this page actually found when researchers went and checked — hover an outcome box for the citations. ⓘ

↓ yes

↓ no

Is system headroom available?
↓ yes, or can be added cost-effectively
Are marginal expansion costs less than embedded (average) costs?
↓ yes
Can incremental cost be recovered from the new customer/class over the asset lifetime?
↓ yes
Rates are not affected or decrease
Real-world instance: Watten/Bistline/Blanford 2026 (causal IV design, -3.5% to -6%); Brattle/Alliant 2026 (IA/WI, net neutral-to-protective)
Additional factors that widen or narrow the impact (ESIG, Box 2)
  • Load forecast accuracy — how well the timing of the new load lines up with planned system upgrades.
  • Large-load tariff design — cost-allocation practices and tariff terms shape how costs or savings reach existing customers.
  • Customer load profile — how much the load coincides with system peak drives how much capacity must be reserved for it.
  • Distribution system impacts — loads connecting at distribution voltage may need dedicated substation/feeder upgrades.
  • Economic development rates — incentive rates can create a gap between the cost of serving a load and the revenue it pays.
  • Frequency of rate cases — costs added between rate cases aren't reflected in rates until the next proceeding.
  • Multi-year rate plans — regulatory lag can delay when a large load's rate impact actually shows up.
  • Other load growth — growth from other customers dilutes the per-unit impact of any single large-load class.
